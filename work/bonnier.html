Case Study

Di Investor Relations: Service design for the Nordic’s biggest news publication

A complete IR video solution for Di.se

My Role

Product strategy, Competitive research, UX design

Team

1 PM, 3 developers, 1 UX designer (me) + client team

Timeline

April 2025 – April 2026

Output

A pipeline for live-broadcasting quarterly reports, built across four products plus three components integrated into Di.se

Background Guiding principle Design decisions What we left out Impact What I learned

Background

Investors were moving from PDFs to video. Quarterly reports were increasingly consumed as CEO interviews and live presentations, and a Finnish platform had already built a strong position across the Nordics.

Dagens Industri had everything needed to compete – the Nordic’s biggest business audience, its own studios, and the trust of listed companies. What it didn’t have was the technology. That’s where we came in.

We pitched a partnership: Di brings the audience and the brand, my company builds and runs the service behind it. One year later, it launched as Di Investor Relations.

How the service works

Behind every quarterly report broadcast are five roles, each with a different job.

Role Who What they do
Operator My team Sets up and runs the broadcast
Speakers CEO, CFO Presents the report and answers questions during the broadcast
Participants Analysts, experts, investors Asks questions live (desktop)
Callers Analysts, experts, investors Asks questions live (phone)
Viewers Di.se visitors Watches the broadcast on Di.se
Company books a broadcast
→
Operator sets up stream, signup forms and slides
→
Speakers and participants meet in the broadcast
Viewers watch on Di.se and the company’s investor page
→
The video stays in the archive

Our research showed that nobody – neither the listed companies nor Bonnier – wanted to run a technical platform themselves. So the service was designed as a package, with my company operating every broadcast as part of the contract.

The guiding principle: Seamless

Behind the scenes, the service runs on four products, two brands and five roles. In front, it should feel like one thing: a company holds a broadcast, and it appears where its investors are already reading.

Every design decision aimed to hide the seams – between products, between Qbrick and Di, and between the people taking part.

Design decisions

Every role gets its own way in

A CEO about to present, an analyst with a question and a reader on Di.se have nothing in common except the broadcast. Instead of one shared meeting experience, like Teams or Zoom, each role gets its own entry point and its own view.

Example

Four ways into the same broadcast

Speakers get a direct link from the operator. Participants fill in a form and receive an invitation by email. Callers use the same form, but get a phone number to dial. Viewers sign up next to the player, wherever it’s embedded.

One list for the operator

However people join, they end up in the same participants list (except for viewers), tagged and filterable by role. The operator can find someone with a question in seconds, and mute, unmute or move people between rooms.

A speaker sees only what they need

The CEO sees their colleagues and the presentation – and can change slides themselves.

Operator’s view in the live management tool — participants list

Di in front, our design behind

Readers should never notice that another company is powering the video. Everything the audience sees – widgets, player, forms, emails – is branded as Dagens Industri. Everything the operator uses stays in my company’s platform.

Example

Designing for Di.se without Di’s design system

For the first nine months, we didn’t have access to Bonnier’s design system or team. I studied Di.se’s live site and rebuilt its design language component by component. When Bonnier’s UX team joined, they translated my designs into their system – the final components match about 80%.

Video channels in two layers

Participating companies also show their videos on their own investor pages. I redesigned our video channels in two layers: the structure (grid, hierarchy, video components) stays locked to the Di look, while logo, colours and details follow each company’s brand.

Video on company’s website with slides and chat

Build on whatever works

With one year and no existing webcast tool, building everything from scratch wasn’t an option. So we built on what already worked – ours and others’.

Example

Open source video conference tool

Instead of building a video meeting tool, we integrated an open-source one, Jitsi Meet, into our live streaming platform. We got webcams, invitations, screen sharing and chat for free, and added our own layer on top: the participants list, moderation and slides. Today, all of my company’s customers can use it – not just Bonnier.

Slides inside the player

Our competitor showed video and slides side by side on a full page – which can’t be embedded and works poorly on mobile. We built the slides into our own video player instead, so the presentation works on any screen and anywhere the player is embedded.

Presenter view — video, slides and participants in one place

Make every setup quick

Each broadcast needs two forms – one for viewers, one for participants – and several emails each. The operator would be doing this every earnings season.

Example

Smart defaults

Fields come pre-filled with neutral text that works for most events. A language picker translates every field at once. Dynamic tags fill in the event name and date automatically, so nothing needs updating by hand if the schedule changes.

Email setup with smart defaults and dynamic tags

What we left out

Setting up a broadcast means moving through several products in a fixed order: creating the live event, adding forms, inviting speakers, uploading slides. A creation wizard would have tied it together, but it didn’t fit the deadline. Since the operators were our own team, we accepted the friction for version 1.

What I learned

Cross-team projects have to be cross-team from the start. Bonnier’s team joined nine months into a twelve-month project, so late ideas and problems surfaced exactly when we had the least time to handle them. In a partnership like this, I would push for both teams to be in the room from the first design meeting.
